## 💡 Core Intuition: The Clock Face & Turning Backwards by Moving Forwards

Why does every microprocessor on Earth use **complements** instead of ordinary subtraction?

Building physical silicon subtraction circuits (borrow logic, borrow-propagation trees) requires almost twice the silicon real estate of an adder, drastically increasing chip die area, parasitic capacitance, and power consumption. Complements allow the CPU to perform **subtraction using purely additive hardware**!

### 🍳 The Everyday Analogy: The 12-Hour Wall Clock

Suppose your watch displays 9:00, and you need to set it back by 4 hours ($9 - 4 = 5$). You have two equivalent choices:
1. **Rotate the dial counter-clockwise** by 4 hours: $9 - 4 = 5$.
2. **Rotate the dial clockwise** by 8 hours: $9 + 8 = 17 \equiv 5 \pmod{12}$.

$$
-4 \equiv +8 \pmod{12}
$$

Because the clock dial wraps around modulo 12, **subtracting 4 is mathematically identical to adding 8** (the 12's complement of 4)! 

In digital hardware with an $n$-bit register, numbers naturally wrap around modulo $2^n$. By substituting negative numbers with their radix complement, the Arithmetic Logic Unit (ALU) performs subtractions using the exact same silicon adder circuits that execute standard additions!

### 💻 Bridging to Computer Science

In processor architecture and compiler runtimes:
1. **Unified Arithmetic Hardware**: An ALU does not need separate adder and subtractor silicon circuits. Subtraction $A - B$ is executed as $A + (\bar{B} + 1)$ on the exact same adder datapath.
2. **Elimination of Negative Zero**: 1's complement has two representations for zero ($+0 = 0000_2$ and $-0 = 1111_2$), requiring extra logic in conditional branches (`if (x == 0)`). 2's complement has exactly one unique zero ($0000_2$), simplifying CPU control logic.
3. **Asymmetric Dynamic Range**: In an $n$-bit 2's complement system, the range is $[-2^{n-1}, +(2^{n-1} - 1)]$. The extra negative value (e.g. $-128$ in 8-bit) explains why integer negation can overflow in C/C++ (`abs(INT_MIN)` undefined behavior).

## 📐 Mathematical Foundations of Complements

For any positional number system with base (radix) $r$, there exist two distinct families of complements:
1. **The Radix Complement ($r$'s complement)**:
   * In Decimal ($r=10$): **10's complement**
   * In Binary ($r=2$): **2's complement**
2. **The Diminished Radix Complement ($(r-1)$'s complement)**:
   * In Decimal ($r=10$): **9's complement**
   * In Binary ($r=2$): **1's complement**

The fundamental identity linking the two families is:

<MathBox title="Fundamental Complement Relationship" type="formula">

$$
\text{Radix Complement } [r\text{'s}] = \text{Diminished Radix Complement } [(r-1)\text{'s}] + 1
$$

</MathBox>

---

## 🔟 Diminished Radix Complement: $(r-1)$'s Complement

Given an $n$-digit integer $N$ in radix $r$:

<MathBox title="Diminished Radix Complement Definition" type="formula">

$$
(r-1)\text{'s Complement of } N = (r^n - 1) - N
$$

If $N$ contains $m$ fractional digits:
$$
(r-1)\text{'s Complement of } N = (r^n - r^{-m}) - N
$$

</MathBox>

Notice that $(r^n - 1)$ is simply a number consisting of $n$ repetitions of the **maximum digit** $(r-1)$!
* In **Decimal ($r=10$)**, $10^4 - 1 = 9999$. To find the 9's complement, simply subtract every digit from $9$.
* In **Binary ($r=2$)**, $2^4 - 1 = 15 = (1111)_2$. To find the 1's complement, simply subtract every bit from $1$.

### Decimal 9's Complement Example
Find the 9's complement of $(546700)_{10}$ ($n=6$ digits):
$$
\begin{aligned}
9\text{'s Comp} &= 999999 - 546700 = \mathbf{453299}
\end{aligned}
$$

### Binary 1's Complement Example
Find the 1's complement of $(10110100)_2$ ($n=8$ bits):
$$
\begin{aligned}
1\text{'s Comp} &= 11111111 - 10110100 = \mathbf{01001011}_2
\end{aligned}
$$
Notice the physical rule: **Subtracting a binary bit from 1 simply inverts the bit ($1 - 0 = 1$ and $1 - 1 = 0$)!**
In silicon, 1's complement is implemented with zero calculation: simply pass each bit through a single NOT gate inverter!

## ⚡ Radix Complement: $r$'s Complement

Given an $n$-digit integer $N$ in radix $r$ (with $N \ne 0$):

<MathBox title="Radix Complement Definition" type="formula">

$$
r\text{'s Complement of } N = r^n - N = \big[(r-1)\text{'s Complement}\big] + 1
$$

*(For $N=0$, the $r$'s complement is defined as $0$.)*

</MathBox>

### Decimal 10's Complement Example
Find the 10's complement of $(546700)_{10}$:
* **Method 1 (Definition)**: $10^6 - 546700 = 1000000 - 546700 = \mathbf{453300}$.
* **Method 2 ($(r-1)\text{'s} + 1$)**: $9\text{'s Comp} + 1 = 453299 + 1 = \mathbf{453300}$.

### Binary 2's Complement Example
Find the 2's complement of $(10110100)_2$:
* **Method 1**: Invert all bits (1's comp) and add 1:
  $$
  10110100 \xrightarrow{\text{Invert}} 01001011 \xrightarrow{+1} \mathbf{01001100}_2
  $$

## 🚀 Fast Silicon Shortcuts (Bit Inversion & RTL Scan)

In high-speed hardware and competitive timed exams, computing $(r-1)\text{'s} + 1$ with carry ripple is slow. Two high-speed shortcuts exist:

### The Right-to-Left (RTL) Bit Scanning Shortcut
To find the 2's complement of any binary vector:
1. Scan the bits from **Right to Left (LSB toward MSB)**.
2. Leave all trailing zeros **unchanged**.
3. Leave the **first '1' bit encountered unchanged**.
4. **Invert every bit to the left** of that first '1' ($0 \to 1, \, 1 \to 0$)!

### Case 1: Subtraction when Minuend Exceeds Subtrahend (Positive Result)

When the minuend $M$ is greater than or equal to the subtrahend $N$ ($M \ge N$), the true arithmetic difference $M - N$ is non-negative ($\ge 0$). In hardware complement arithmetic, adding the complement of $N$ generates an overflow end carry ($C_{out} = 1$), confirming that the result is strictly positive and requires no secondary complementation.

#### A. Using 2's Complement ($r$'s complement)
1. Compute $M + (2\text{'s Complement of } N)$.
2. An **End Carry-Out ($C_{out} = 1$) will be generated**.
3. **Discard the End Carry!** The remaining bits represent the exact positive answer.

**Problem**: Compute $(25)_{10} - (14)_{10}$ in 8-bit binary.
* $M = 25 = 00011001_2$
* $N = 14 = 00001110_2$
* 2's Comp of $N = 11110001 + 1 = 11110010_2$

$$
\begin{array}{rl}
  00011001 & (25) \\
+ 11110010 & (-14 \text{ in 2's comp}) \\
\hline
\mathbf{1} \, 00001011 & \\
\end{array}
$$
* End Carry $= 1 \implies$ **Discard carry!**
* Result $= 00001011_2 = \mathbf{+11}_{10}$. *(Correct!)*

---

#### B. Using 1's Complement ($(r-1)$'s complement)
1. Compute $M + (1\text{'s Complement of } N)$.
2. An **End Carry-Out ($C_{out} = 1$) will be generated**.
3. **Add the Carry to the Least Significant Bit (LSB)**! This is called the **End-Around Carry (EAC)**.

**Problem**: Compute $(25)_{10} - (14)_{10}$ in 8-bit binary using 1's complement.
* $M = 25 = 00011001_2$
* $1\text{'s Comp of } N = 11110001_2$

$$
\begin{array}{rl}
  00011001 & (25) \\
+ 11110001 & (1\text{'s comp of } 14) \\
\hline
\mathbf{1} \, 00001010 & (\text{End Carry } = 1) \\
         + 1 & (\text{End-Around Carry added to LSB}) \\
\hline
  \mathbf{00001011} & = \mathbf{+11}_{10} \quad \text{(Correct!)}
\end{array}
$$

---

### Case 2: Subtraction when Subtrahend Exceeds Minuend (Negative Result)

When $M < N$, adding the complement does **not generate an End Carry ($C_{out} = 0$)**.
* The absence of an end carry indicates that the result is **negative**.
* The bit pattern obtained is **not** the true magnitude; it is **in complement form**.
* To obtain the true magnitude, take the complement of the sum and prefix a minus sign!

#### Example: Compute $(14)_{10} - (25)_{10}$ in 8-bit binary using 2's complement
* $M = 14 = 00001110_2$
* $N = 25 = 00011001_2$
* 2's Comp of $N = 11100110 + 1 = 11100111_2$

$$
\begin{array}{rl}
  00001110 & (14) \\
+ 11100111 & (2\text{'s comp of } 25) \\
\hline
\mathbf{0} \, 11110101 & (\text{No End Carry: } C_{out} = 0!)
\end{array}
$$
* No End Carry $\implies$ Result is **negative** and stored in 2's complement form ($11110101_2$).
* Take 2's complement of $11110101_2$:
  $$
  11110101 \xrightarrow{\text{2's Comp}} 00001010 + 1 = 00001011_2 = (11)_{10}
  $$
* Final Result: $\mathbf{-11}_{10}$. *(Correct!)*

* **The Unified Adder-Subtractor**: Modern CPUs (x86, ARM, RISC-V) perform subtraction with zero added adder hardware. A bank of XOR gates conditionally inverts input $B$ when subtraction mode ($M=1$) is asserted, while feeding $C_{in} = 1$ into the LSB full adder.
* **Instruction Set Encoding**: All modern programming languages (C, C++, Rust, Go, Java, Python) standardize on 2's complement for signed integer types (`int8_t`, `int16_t`, `int32_t`, `int64_t`), ensuring hardware-level binary compatibility.

## 📐 Numerical Applications & Worked Examples

### Example 1: Decimal Subtraction Using 9's and 10's Complements (Both Cases)
**Problem Statement:**  
Perform the subtraction of 5-digit decimal numbers $M = 72532$ and $N = 03250$:
1. Compute $M - N$ using 10's complement (Case 1: $M \ge N$).
2. Compute $N - M$ using 10's complement (Case 2: $M < N$).

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
1. **Case 1: Compute $M - N = 72532 - 03250$ using 10's Complement:**  
   * 9's complement of $N = 03250$: $99999 - 03250 = 96749$  
   * 10's complement of $N$: $96749 + 1 = 96750$  
   * Add $M + (10\text{'s comp of } N)$:

$$
\begin{array}{rl}
  & 72532 \\
+ & 96750 \\
\hline
\mathbf{1} & 69282 \quad (C_{out} = 1 \text{ generated})
\end{array}
$$

   * An End Carry-Out ($C_{out} = 1$) is generated, indicating $M \ge N$ (result is positive).  
   * **Discard the end carry**: Final Answer = $\mathbf{+69282}$.

2. **Case 2: Compute $N - M = 03250 - 72532$ using 10's Complement:**  
   * 9's complement of $M = 72532$: $99999 - 72532 = 27467$  
   * 10's complement of $M$: $27467 + 1 = 27468$  
   * Add $N + (10\text{'s comp of } M)$:

$$
\begin{array}{rl}
  & 03250 \\
+ & 27468 \\
\hline
\mathbf{0} & 30718 \quad (C_{out} = 0 \text{ no carry})
\end{array}
$$

   * No carry generated ($C_{out} = 0$), indicating $M < N$ (result is negative and in 10's complement form).  
   * Take 10's complement of the sum $30718$: $99999 - 30718 = 69281 \implies 69281 + 1 = 69282$.  
   * Prepend negative sign: Final Answer = $\mathbf{-69282}$.

### Example 2: 1's vs 2's Complement Subtraction in 6-Bit Binary
**Problem Statement:**  
Perform $(28)_{10} - (13)_{10}$ and $(13)_{10} - (28)_{10}$ in 6-bit binary arithmetic using:
1. 1's complement representation.
2. 2's complement representation.

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
1. **Represent Numbers in 6-Bit Binary:**  
   * $M = 28_{10} = 011100_2$  
   * $N = 13_{10} = 001101_2$
2. **Compute $28 - 13$ in 1's Complement:**  
   * 1's complement of $N$: invert all bits $\implies 110010_2$.  
   * Add $M + N_{1\text{'s}}$:  
     $$011100 + 110010 = \mathbf{1}\,001110 \quad (C_{out} = 1)$$  
   * End carry generated! In 1's complement, add End-Around Carry (EAC) to LSB:  
     $$001110 + 1 = \mathbf{001111}_2 = +15_{10}$$
3. **Compute $28 - 13$ in 2's Complement:**  
   * 2's complement of $N = 110010 + 1 = 110011_2$.  
   * Add $M + N_{2\text{'s}}$:  
     $$011100 + 110011 = \mathbf{1}\,001111 \quad (C_{out} = 1)$$  
   * End carry generated! In 2's complement, **discard carry**:  
     $$\text{Result} = \mathbf{001111}_2 = +15_{10}$$ (Notice: instantaneous zero extra gate delay!).
4. **Compute $13 - 28$ in 2's Complement:**  
   * 2's complement of $M = (011100)_{2\text{'s}} = 100011 + 1 = 100100_2$.  
   * Add $N + M_{2\text{'s}}$:  
     $$001101 + 100100 = \mathbf{0}\,110001 \quad (C_{out} = 0)$$  
   * No carry generated $\implies$ negative result in 2's complement form.  
   * Take 2's complement of sum $110001$:  
     $$\text{Invert} = 001110 \implies +1 = 001111_2 = 15_{10}$$  
   * Final Answer: $\mathbf{-001111}_2 = -15_{10}$.

### Example 3: Complements of Fractional Numbers & Radix Extension
**Problem Statement:**  
Find the diminished radix complement and radix complement for the fractional number $N = (0.3267)_{10}$ and binary fraction $B = (0.10110)_2$. State the generalized mathematical formula for fractional complements.

**Step-by-Step Solution:**
1. **Generalized Mathematical Formulas for Fractions:**  
   For a number with $n$ integer digits and $m$ fractional digits:  
   * Diminished Radix Complement:  
     $$(r-1)\text{'s Complement} = (r^n - r^{-m}) - N$$  
   * Radix Complement:  
     $$r\text{'s Complement} = (r-1)\text{'s Complement} + r^{-m} = r^n - N$$
2. **Compute for Decimal Fraction $N = 0.3267_{10}$ ($n=0, m=4$):**  
   * 9's complement: subtract each digit from $9$:  
     $$0.9999 - 0.3267 = \mathbf{0.6732}$$  
   * 10's complement: add $10^{-m} = 0.0001$ to LSB:  
     $$0.6732 + 0.0001 = \mathbf{0.6733}$$  
   * Direct verification: $10^0 - 0.3267 = 1.0000 - 0.3267 = 0.6733$.
3. **Compute for Binary Fraction $B = 0.10110_2$ ($n=0, m=5$):**  
   * 1's complement: invert all bits after the binary point:  
     $$0.10110 \implies \mathbf{0.01001}_2$$  
   * 2's complement: add $2^{-5} = 0.00001_2$ to LSB:  
     $$0.01001 + 0.00001 = \mathbf{0.01010}_2$$  
   * Direct verification via RTL scan shortcut: scanning from right, trailing zero is $0$, first 1 is $1$, all bits to the left are inverted $\implies \mathbf{0.01010}_2$.

## 🎯 Exam & Interview Pitfall Check

:::tip Core Conceptual Questions
* **Why does 2's complement have one extra negative number than positive numbers?**  
  In an $n$-bit system, there are $2^n$ unique bit combinations. Zero ($0000\dots0$) takes one combination, leaving $2^n - 1$ states (an odd number). Because zero is assigned to the non-negative group (its MSB is $0$), there are $2^{n-1} - 1$ positive numbers ($+1$ to $+2^{n-1}-1$) and $2^{n-1}$ negative numbers ($-1$ to $-2^{n-1}$).
* **What happens if you take the 2's complement of the most negative number (e.g. $-128$ in 8-bit)?**  
  In 8-bit 2's complement, $-128$ is represented as $10000000_2$.  
  Inverting bits yields $01111111_2$.  
  Adding 1 yields: $01111111 + 1 = 10000000_2$ (which is $-128$ again!).  
  Because $+128$ cannot fit in 8-bit signed two's complement, the operation triggers an **Arithmetic Overflow**!
:::

:::warning Common Interview Traps
* **Trap: Forgetting that End-Around Carry applies ONLY to 1's complement, NEVER 2's complement.**  
  In 2's complement subtraction, if an end carry appears, **throw it away**. Never add it back to the LSB!
* **Trap: Misidentifying the result of Case 2 ($M < N$).**  
  When $C_{out} = 0$, students frequently read the bit pattern directly as the magnitude. Remember: the sum is in **complement form**. You must complement the sum to obtain the true magnitude, then write a negative sign in front!
:::
