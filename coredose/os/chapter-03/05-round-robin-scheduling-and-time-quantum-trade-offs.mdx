### 💻 Bridging to Computer Science

In operating systems, **Round Robin (RR)** is designed specifically for **time-sharing and interactive multi-user systems**.
* The Ready Queue is treated as a **circular FIFO queue**.
* The scheduler fixes a **Time Quantum ($q$)** (also called a time slice), typically ranging from $10\text{ ms}$ to $100\text{ ms}$.
* The CPU executes each process for up to one time quantum. If the process does not terminate or block within that window, a hardware timer interrupt fires, the process is preempted, and its PCB is returned to the tail of the Ready Queue.
* Essentially: **Round Robin is FCFS with preemption added**.

## 📚 Core Deep-Dive & Concepts

### 1. Round Robin Architectural Principles & Queue Bound Theorem

> **Definition**: Round Robin is designed for time-sharing systems where it is not necessary to complete one process and then start another, but to be responsive and divide CPU time equitably among processes in the ready state. The ready queue is managed as a circular queue.

#### The Ready Queue Bound Theorem
If there are $n$ processes in the Ready Queue and the time quantum is $q$:
1. Each process receives $\mathbf{\frac{1}{n}}$ of the CPU time in chunks of at most $q$ time units.
2. Each process must wait no longer than $\mathbf{(n - 1) \cdot q}$ time units until its next time quantum.

$$\mathbf{\text{Maximum Inter-Dispatch Delay} = (n - 1) \cdot q}$$

For example, with $5$ active processes and a time quantum of $20\text{ ms}$, no process ever waits longer than $(5 - 1) \times 20\text{ ms} = 80\text{ ms}$ before getting the CPU again.

### 2. Comprehensive Problem: 6-Process Step-by-Step Derivation ($q = 2\text{ ms}$)

Consider six processes scheduled under Round Robin with a **Time Quantum $q = 2\text{ ms}$**:

| Process ID | Arrival Time ($AT$) | Burst Time ($BT$) |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $P_0$ | $0\text{ ms}$ | $4\text{ ms}$ |
| $P_1$ | $1\text{ ms}$ | $5\text{ ms}$ |
| $P_2$ | $2\text{ ms}$ | $2\text{ ms}$ |
| $P_3$ | $3\text{ ms}$ | $1\text{ ms}$ |
| $P_4$ | $4\text{ ms}$ | $6\text{ ms}$ |
| $P_5$ | $6\text{ ms}$ | $3\text{ ms}$ |

#### Standard Queue Enqueue Rule:
Whenever a process's quantum expires at timestamp $t$ simultaneously with the arrival of new processes at timestamp $t$, **newly arriving processes are enqueued into the Ready Queue before the preempted process is re-enqueued at the tail**.

#### Step-by-Step Ready Queue Evolution:
* **$t = 0$**: Ready Queue: $[P_0]$. $P_0$ is dispatched for $q = 2\text{ ms}$ ($t = 0 \to 2$).
  * $P_0$ remaining burst $= 4 - 2 = 2\text{ ms}$.
  * During $[0, 2]$, $P_1$ arrived at $t = 1$, and $P_2$ arrived at $t = 2$.
  * Queue order at $t = 2$: $[P_1, P_2, P_0]$.
* **$t = 2$**: Head process $P_1$ runs for $q = 2\text{ ms}$ ($t = 2 \to 4$).
  * $P_1$ remaining burst $= 5 - 2 = 3\text{ ms}$.
  * During $[2, 4]$, $P_3$ arrived at $t = 3$, and $P_4$ arrived at $t = 4$.
  * Queue order at $t = 4$: $[P_2, P_0, P_3, P_4, P_1]$.
* **$t = 4$**: Head process $P_2$ runs for $q = 2\text{ ms}$ ($t = 4 \to 6$).
  * $P_2$ remaining burst $= 2 - 2 = 0\text{ ms}$.
  * **$P_2$ completes and terminates at $t = 6$ ($CT = 6$)!**
  * Meanwhile, $P_5$ arrives at $t = 6$.
  * Queue order at $t = 6$: $[P_0, P_3, P_4, P_1, P_5]$.
* **$t = 6$**: Head process $P_0$ runs for its remaining $2\text{ ms}$ ($t = 6 \to 8$).
  * $P_0$ remaining burst $= 2 - 2 = 0\text{ ms}$.
  * **$P_0$ completes and terminates at $t = 8$ ($CT = 8$)!**
  * Queue order at $t = 8$: $[P_3, P_4, P_1, P_5]$.
* **$t = 8$**: Head process $P_3$ runs. $P_3$'s total burst is $1\text{ ms} < q$.
  * $P_3$ executes for $1\text{ ms}$ ($t = 8 \to 9$) and **terminates at $t = 9$ ($CT = 9$)!**
  * Queue order at $t = 9$: $[P_4, P_1, P_5]$.
* **$t = 9$**: Head process $P_4$ runs for $q = 2\text{ ms}$ ($t = 9 \to 11$).
  * $P_4$ remaining burst $= 6 - 2 = 4\text{ ms}$.
  * Queue order at $t = 11$: $[P_1, P_5, P_4]$.
* **$t = 11$**: Head process $P_1$ runs for $q = 2\text{ ms}$ ($t = 11 \to 13$).
  * $P_1$ remaining burst $= 3 - 2 = 1\text{ ms}$.
  * Queue order at $t = 13$: $[P_5, P_4, P_1]$.
* **$t = 13$**: Head process $P_5$ runs for $q = 2\text{ ms}$ ($t = 13 \to 15$).
  * $P_5$ remaining burst $= 3 - 2 = 1\text{ ms}$.
  * Queue order at $t = 15$: $[P_4, P_1, P_5]$.
* **$t = 15$**: Head process $P_4$ runs for $q = 2\text{ ms}$ ($t = 15 \to 17$).
  * $P_4$ remaining burst $= 4 - 2 = 2\text{ ms}$.
  * Queue order at $t = 17$: $[P_1, P_5, P_4]$.
* **$t = 17$**: Head process $P_1$ runs for its final $1\text{ ms} < q$ ($t = 17 \to 18$).
  * **$P_1$ completes and terminates at $t = 18$ ($CT = 18$)!**
  * Queue order at $t = 18$: $[P_5, P_4]$.
* **$t = 18$**: Head process $P_5$ runs for its final $1\text{ ms} < q$ ($t = 18 \to 19$).
  * **$P_5$ completes and terminates at $t = 19$ ($CT = 19$)!**
  * Queue order at $t = 19$: $[P_4]$.
* **$t = 19$**: Only $P_4$ remains. $P_4$ runs its final $2\text{ ms}$ ($t = 19 \to 21$).
  * **$P_4$ completes and terminates at $t = 21$ ($CT = 21$)!**
* **$t = 21$**: All six processes finished.

### 3. Context Switch Overhead & The Time Quantum Equation

In real hardware, switching the CPU from one process to another is not instantaneous. The OS must save CPU registers to the outgoing process PCB, update memory mapping tables, and restore registers from the incoming PCB. Let this overhead be **$s$** (context switch latency).

#### Mathematical Formulation for Maximum Time Quantum:
If $n$ processes are active, each process context-switch consumes $s$ time units, and the system must guarantee a maximum response time bound of $t$:

$$\mathbf{q \le \frac{t - n \cdot s}{n - 1}}$$

Where:
* $q$: Maximum permissible time quantum.
* $t$: Required maximum response time tolerance.
* $n$: Total number of active processes in the Ready Queue.
* $s$: Context switch time overhead.

#### The 80% Rule of Thumb:
In production operating system engineering, empirical benchmarking demonstrates that **the time quantum should be chosen such that approximately $80\%$ of CPU bursts are shorter than $q$**. This ensures the majority of processes execute to I/O completion within a single slice without triggering preemption interrupts.

## 🏭 In The Real World: Production Case Study

### Linux CFS Latency Emulation vs. POSIX `SCHED_RR`

While desktop Linux uses the Completely Fair Scheduler (CFS) for standard tasks, it provides native Round Robin scheduling under the **POSIX `SCHED_RR`** real-time policy:

```c
// Setting a real-time thread to POSIX Round Robin
#include <sched.h>

struct sched_param param;
param.sched_priority = 50; // Real-time priority (1 - 99)

sched_setscheduler(0, SCHED_RR, &param);
```

#### Production Characteristics:
1. **Real-Time Priority Preemption**: Threads running under `SCHED_RR` run at fixed real-time priorities (1–99) that preempt all normal CFS tasks.
2. **Deterministic Round Robin**: Among equal-priority real-time threads, Linux schedules them via Round Robin with a quantum inspectable via:
   ```bash
   cat /proc/sys/kernel/sched_rr_timeslice_ms
   # Default: 100 ms
   ```
3. **Usage in Telecommunications & Audio Drivers**: Used for DSP audio synthesis pipelines and industrial robotics where every actuator loop requires a guaranteed slice every $10\text{ ms}$ without jitter.

## 🎯 Exam & Interview Pitfall Check

:::tip Core Conceptual Questions

**Question 1:** Prove what happens to Round Robin scheduling when the Time Quantum ($q$) becomes extremely large, and what happens when it becomes extremely small.
**Answer:**
1. **When $q \to \infty$ ($q > \text{all burst times}$)**: Every process finishes its CPU burst within its very first allocated slice without being preempted. The algorithm becomes identical to **First-Come, First-Served (FCFS)** scheduling.
2. **When $q \to 0$ (extremely small)**: Known as **Processor Sharing**. In theory, $n$ processes appear to run concurrently, each at $\frac{1}{n}$ of processor speed. In practice, the system collapses due to **context-switch thrashing**, where the CPU burns virtually all clock cycles swapping hardware registers rather than executing instructions.

**Question 2:** State the Ready Queue Bound Theorem for Round Robin scheduling.
**Answer:**
If there are $n$ processes in the Ready Queue and the time quantum is $q$:
- Each process receives $\frac{1}{n}$ of CPU time in slices of at most $q$ units.
- No process waits longer than $(n - 1) \cdot q$ time units between successive CPU dispatches.

:::

:::warning Common Interview Traps

* **The "Simultaneous Arrival vs. Preemption Re-enqueue" Trap**: In exam problems, when a process's quantum finishes at $t = 4$ and a new process arrives at $t = 4$, candidates frequently re-enqueue the preempted process *before* the new arrival. Always enqueue **new arrivals into the Ready Queue first**, and then append the preempted process to the tail.
* **The "Quantum Greater than Remaining Burst" Misconception**: If process $P_3$ needs $1\text{ ms}$ of burst time and the quantum is $q = 2\text{ ms}$, the CPU does **not** sit idle for the unused $1\text{ ms}$. $P_3$ executes for $1\text{ ms}$, voluntarily yields upon termination, and the scheduler dispatches the next process immediately at $t + 1$.
* **Confusing Average Turnaround with SJF**: While Round Robin provides the best *response time*, its average *turnaround time* is often worse than SJF or SRTF because short jobs take multiple rounds to complete.
:::
